Chapter 1 · The Physical Floor

Electricity & Electronics

Before a single bit can move anywhere, someone has to push an electron. Every layer this site covers — packets, protocols, data centers, the whole Internet — is built on top of the physics in this chapter.

15–20 min read Prerequisites: none Chapter 1 of 12

What's actually moving inside a wire?

Every atom has a nucleus surrounded by electrons — tiny, negatively charged particles. In most materials, those electrons stay put, bound tightly to their own atom. But in a handful of materials, called conductors (copper and aluminum are the ones you'll meet most), the outermost electrons aren't bound to any single atom. They roam freely through the material's atomic lattice, jostling around at random — until something gives them a reason to move in one direction.

That "something" is voltage: a difference in electrical potential between two points, created by a battery, a power supply, or another source. Voltage doesn't push electrons by magic — think of it as a difference in electrical "pressure" between two ends of a wire. Wherever that pressure exists, free electrons drift from the low-potential end toward the high-potential end. The rate at which that charge flows past a point is current, measured in amperes (amps).

Analogy: a circuit is a city grid

Picture current as cars and voltage as the slope of the road. A steep hill (high voltage) pushes more cars downhill per second (more current) than a gentle slope. A traffic jam — resistance — slows the flow no matter how steep the hill is. Every circuit is just this system, closed into a loop: cars have to come back around to go down the hill again, which is exactly why a circuit has to be a complete loop for current to flow at all.

One historical quirk worth knowing: engineers define "conventional current" as flowing from positive to negative, but actual electrons move the opposite way, negative to positive. That backwards convention was set by Benjamin Franklin in the 1700s, decades before anyone knew electrons existed — and it stuck. Every circuit diagram you'll ever see uses it. It doesn't change any of the math, just which direction the arrow points.

Circuits and Ohm's Law: the only equation you truly need

Connect a voltage source to a conductor and current flows — but how much? That depends on resistance: how much a material fights the flow of electrons, measured in ohms (Ω). Insulators like rubber or glass have almost no free electrons, so their resistance is enormous — current barely flows at all, which is exactly why they're used to coat wires. Conductors have low resistance. And the relationship between all three quantities is Ohm's Law: 

V = I × R
(Voltage = Current × Resistance)

This one equation is why every circuit — from a flashlight to a CPU — can be designed on paper before it's built. Say you want to light an LED from a 5V supply. The LED itself drops about 2V across it and wants roughly 20mA (0.02A) of current to glow at normal brightness without burning out. You need a resistor to absorb the remaining voltage: 

R = (5V - 2V) / 0.02A = 150Ω

Drop a 150Ω resistor in series with the LED, and the math guarantees it gets exactly the current it wants. This is the entire discipline of analog circuit design in miniature: pick the current you want, measure the voltage you have, solve for the resistor in between.

Try it yourself: prove Ohm's Law on a breadboard

You'll need a breadboard, a 5V source (a USB power brick works), an LED, a 150Ω resistor, and a multimeter.

  1. Wire the resistor in series with the LED across the 5V supply.
  2. Set your multimeter to measure DC voltage across just the resistor.
  3. You should read close to 3V (5V supply minus the LED's ~2V drop) — confirming the LED is doing exactly what its datasheet promised.
  4. Switch the multimeter to current mode, break the circuit, and measure current in series. You should read close to 20mA.
  5. Now swap in a 330Ω resistor instead. Current should drop to roughly 9mA, and the LED will visibly dim — Ohm's Law, made visible.
Did you know?

Individual electrons in a copper wire drift shockingly slowly — often under a millimeter per second, slower than a snail. Yet flip a light switch across the room and the bulb turns on instantly. That's because you're not waiting for an electron to physically travel from the switch to the bulb; you're waiting for the electric field to propagate through the wire, which happens at a large fraction of the speed of light. The "signal" and the "electrons carrying it" are two very different speeds — a distinction that becomes critical later in this site when we talk about why fiber-optic signals can cross oceans in milliseconds.

Transistors: the switch that made computing possible

Resistors and LEDs get you a flashlight. To get a computer, you need a component that can act as a switch — but one controlled by electricity instead of a human finger, and small and fast enough to flip billions of times a second. That component is the transistor: a semiconductor device that uses a small voltage or current at one terminal to control a much larger current flowing between two other terminals.

Analogy: the transistor as a valve

Imagine a garden hose with a clamp on it, and the clamp is controlled by a second, much thinner hose. A tiny squeeze of water through the thin hose is enough to open or close the clamp, controlling a much larger flow of water through the main hose. That's a transistor: a small control signal (the thin hose) switches a much larger current (the main hose) fully on or fully off. Chain enough of these valves together in the right pattern, and you can build logic — AND, OR, NOT — out of nothing but switches. Chain enough logic together, and you have a processor.

Modern chips don't use one transistor — they use an almost incomprehensible number of them, packed onto a piece of silicon smaller than a fingernail, each one switching on and off billions of times per second. Every layer above this one — every packet, every protocol, every website — ultimately reduces to patterns of transistors switching on and off in exactly this way.
