Chapter 2 · Getting a Signal to Travel

Physical Media

A transistor can switch a signal in nanoseconds. Getting that signal from your laptop to a server on another continent is a completely different problem — one that comes down to three choices: copper, glass, or air.

⏱ 20–25 min read 📋 Prerequisites: Chapter 1 📍 Chapter 2 of 12

Three ways to move a signal

Chapter 1 established the ingredients: a voltage difference pushes current, and a transistor can switch that current on and off billions of times a second. Encode a "1" as one state and a "0" as another, switch fast enough, and you have a bitstream. The only question left is what you push that bitstream through. There are exactly three options in wide use today, and every network on Earth is built from some combination of them:

  • Copper — the bits ride as voltage pulses on a metal wire.
  • Fiber optic glass — the bits ride as pulses of light.
  • Radio waves — the bits ride as variations in an electromagnetic field through open air.

Each medium trades off distance, speed, cost, and reliability differently. Understanding those trade-offs explains almost every physical design decision in networking — why your office has Ethernet jacks, why undersea cables are glass and not copper, and why your phone still needs a cell tower even though it's "wireless."

Copper: the original data highway

An Ethernet cable is really four twisted pairs of copper wire bundled together. Each pair carries a differential signal — the two wires carry mirror-image voltages, and the receiver reads the difference between them. This matters because any electrical noise from the environment tends to hit both wires in a pair equally, and equal noise on both wires cancels out when you subtract one from the other. That's the entire reason the wires are twisted: it keeps the pair close enough together that outside interference affects both wires almost identically.

But copper has a hard limit: the signal weakens (attenuates) and timing degrades the farther it travels, which is why Ethernet cabling standards cap out around 100 meters for gigabit speeds (1000BASE-T, over Cat5e or Cat6 cable). Push for higher speeds over copper and the usable distance shrinks — 10-gigabit Ethernet (10GBASE-T) over Cat6 is typically only reliable out to around 55 meters, because faster signaling is more sensitive to the same attenuation and crosstalk that 100 meters of cable barely tolerates at 1 Gbps.

💧 Analogy: copper is a pipe with friction

Push water through a garden hose and pressure drops the farther it travels — friction against the hose walls eats a little energy every meter. A voltage pulse in copper loses a little energy every meter too. Push the "water" faster (higher data rate) and that friction matters more, so the hose has to get shorter for the pressure to still make sense at the far end. That's the entire reason 10-gigabit copper reaches half as far as gigabit copper.

🧪 Try it yourself: measure real copper throughput

Connect two machines directly with an Ethernet cable (or through a switch) and measure actual throughput with iperf3: 

# On machine A (server):
iperf3 -s

# On machine B (client):
iperf3 -c <machine-A-ip>

On a healthy gigabit link you should see throughput close to 940 Mbps — never the full 1000 Mbps, because some bandwidth is always spent on framing overhead. Try it again over a longer or cheaper cable and watch the number drop as attenuation and noise increase.

Fiber optics: light in a glass thread

Fiber-optic cable abandons electricity entirely. Instead, a laser fires pulses of light down a hair-thin strand of glass. The glass core is surrounded by a cladding layer with a different refractive index, which keeps light bouncing down the middle of the fiber through total internal reflection — the same optical effect that lets you see your reflection in a pool of water at the right angle, just engineered on purpose.

Light barely attenuates in glass compared to how quickly voltage attenuates in copper, so a single fiber strand can carry a signal for tens of kilometers without amplification — and with amplifying "repeater" stations, for thousands. That single property is why every long-distance and undersea connection on the planet is fiber, not copper: copper simply can't go the distance without the signal dying.

⚡ Did you know?

Light does travel slower inside fiber than it does in a vacuum — glass has a refractive index of about 1.5, so light moves at roughly 200,000 km/s instead of light speed's usual 300,000 km/s. That still means a signal from New York to London along the shortest undersea route (about 5,600 km) has a physical minimum latency of around 28 milliseconds one-way — a hard floor set by the speed of light itself, no amount of engineering can beat it, only approach it. Real-world latency is usually a bit higher, because the cable route isn't a straight line and the signal passes through amplifiers and switching equipment along the way.

🚇 Analogy: fiber is a mirrored tunnel

Imagine a hallway lined with mirrors on every wall. Shine a flashlight down it at a shallow enough angle and the beam keeps bouncing forward instead of escaping through the walls — it can travel the length of a very long hallway with almost no loss of brightness. A fiber-optic cable is that hallway, engineered at the width of a human hair.

Radio waves: the wireless option

Radio drops the wire entirely and sends the signal as an electromagnetic wave through open space. Different frequency bands within the radio spectrum trade off range against bandwidth in a very consistent way: lower frequencies travel farther and pass through obstacles better, while higher frequencies can carry more data but fall off in range much faster and struggle to penetrate walls.

Bluetooth2.4 GHz~10 m (30 ft)

Notice the pattern: Wi-Fi's 5 GHz band is faster than its 2.4 GHz band precisely because it gives up range to get it, and mmWave 5G pushes that trade-off to an extreme — enormous bandwidth, but a range so short that carriers need a transmitter on nearly every block. It's the same story told three different ways: more bandwidth always costs you something, whether it's a shorter copper run, a fiber's cost of installation, or a radio wave's range.

And here's the detail worth remembering for later chapters: "wireless" almost never means wireless for the whole journey. Your phone's radio signal only has to survive the last few hundred meters to the nearest cell tower or Wi-Fi access point. From there, it drops straight onto fiber for the rest of the trip — which is exactly why Chapter 12 will show that the overwhelming majority of the world's actual data-miles are traveled on glass, not through the air at all.

🧪 Try it yourself: Wi-Fi vs. Ethernet, side by side

Open Wireshark, start a capture on your Wi-Fi interface, and load a large web page. Then plug into Ethernet and repeat the capture on the wired interface.

  1. Compare the TCP retransmissions in each capture — Wi-Fi almost always shows more, since radio is a noisier medium than a shielded cable.
  2. Check round-trip time for the same request on both — Wi-Fi typically adds a few extra milliseconds of jitter.
  3. If your Wi-Fi adapter supports monitor mode, capture raw 802.11 frames with tcpdump -i wlan0mon and notice something Ethernet never needs: management frames for association, authentication, and beacons — the overhead of maintaining a wireless link at all.

What's next

We now have a way to move raw signal — electrical, optical, or radio — from one point to another. But raw signal isn't a network yet: nothing so far has said "this bit is meant for you" versus "this bit is meant for someone else on the same wire." That's the job of the next layer up: Chapter 3 introduces Ethernet frames, MAC addresses, and the switches that turn a shared physical medium into an actual local network.
