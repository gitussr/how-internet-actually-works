Chapter 8 · The Last Few Hundred Meters

Mobile & Cellular Networks

A phone call feels wireless from end to end. In reality, the radio part of that journey usually ends within a few hundred meters — the rest of the trip happens over the exact same fiber backbone covered back in Chapter 2.

15–20 min read Prerequisites: Chapters 2 & 7 Chapter 8 of 12

Cellular architecture: cells, towers, and handover

A cellular network gets its name from how it divides geography: instead of one enormous tower trying to cover an entire city, coverage is split into overlapping cells, each served by its own base station, using frequencies chosen so that neighboring cells don't interfere with each other. Your phone constantly measures signal strength from nearby towers and connects to whichever one currently serves it best.

Your identity on that network doesn't live in the phone itself — it lives on the SIM card, a small chip holding an IMSI (International Mobile Subscriber Identity) that the network uses to recognize you, bill you, and route calls to you. Move the SIM to a different physical phone and your number, plan, and identity move with it, because none of that was ever tied to the hardware in the first place.

The genuinely hard engineering problem is what happens when you're moving — driving down a highway, say, passing from one cell's coverage into the next. The network has to transfer your active connection from one base station to another, called a handover, without dropping the call or losing data mid-transfer, all while your signal strength to the old tower is fading and your signal to the new one is only just becoming usable.

Analogy: handover is a relay race baton pass

Two runners have to overlap their strides so the baton is never dropped and never held by both at once for too long — the handoff has to happen in a narrow window of good timing. A cellular handover is the same problem: the network briefly coordinates with both the old and new tower so your connection transfers cleanly, timed against a signal that's constantly getting weaker on one side and stronger on the other.

Did you know?

A well-executed cellular handover typically completes in well under a second — often close to 100 milliseconds — which is fast enough that almost nobody notices it happening, even on a call made at highway speed, crossing dozens of cell boundaries over the course of a long drive without a single perceptible interruption.

The last hop is wireless. Almost nothing else is.

Once your phone's signal reaches the cell tower, the radio part of the journey is essentially over. From the tower, your data drops onto a wired backhaul connection — usually fiber — straight into the carrier's core network, and from there onto the same Internet backbone every other chapter of this site has described: routers, BGP-negotiated paths, and eventually the same undersea fiber cables Chapter 12 covers in full. "Wireless" describes the first few hundred meters of a journey that, for anything beyond local, is fiber the rest of the way.

2G to 5G: the same job, different trade-offs

Each cellular generation has mostly chased the same frequency trade-offs introduced back in Chapter 2 — more bandwidth in exchange for range and building penetration — while steadily shifting the network's job from carrying voice calls to carrying general-purpose data.

Wi-Fi offload: your phone would rather not use cellular

Given the choice, most phones quietly prefer Wi-Fi over cellular data whenever it's available — and carriers actively encourage this, because a Wi-Fi connection reaches the wired backbone directly through your router, without consuming any cellular tower capacity at all. This is exactly the same wired network from Chapters 2 and 3, just being reached by radio for the last few meters instead of copper — the same fundamental pattern as cellular, at a much smaller scale.

Try it yourself: feel the difference backhaul makes

With a phone that supports tethering, connect a laptop to your phone's cellular hotspot and compare: 

ping -c 20 8.8.8.8    # over cellular tether
ping -c 20 8.8.8.8    # over home Wi-Fi

Cellular latency and jitter are almost always higher and less consistent than a wired-backed Wi-Fi connection — direct evidence of the extra radio coordination (cell selection, handover readiness, scheduling) happening on every cellular packet that a Wi-Fi packet on a stable home network simply doesn't need.
