Why the Internet Actually Works

Right now, something you typed is riding a beam of light under an ocean. This site explains, layer by layer, exactly how that happens — starting from a single electron and ending at the global network of cables, towers, and data centers that make up the Internet.

5–10 min read Prerequisites: none Chapter 0 of 12 Audience: engineers & the curious

Turtles, all the way down — except this time, we can see them

Ask most people how the Internet works and you'll get a shrug, or a hand-wave about "the cloud." Ask a network engineer and you'll get a precise but narrow answer about IP and TCP. Both are missing most of the picture. The Internet isn't one thing — it's about a dozen different engineering miracles, stacked on top of each other, each one invented to solve exactly one problem, each one blissfully ignorant of the layers above and below it.

A transistor doesn't know what a web page is. A fiber-optic cable doesn't know what TCP is. A DNS server doesn't know your packet is about to cross an ocean. And yet, when you type a URL, all of these layers cooperate in milliseconds to get you an answer. That cooperation — total specialization, zero awareness of the whole — is the actual magic trick. This site's entire job is to walk you down through every one of those layers until the trick stops looking like magic.

The analogy we'll keep coming back to

Think of a city. Underground, electricity flows through wires like water through pipes. Above ground, mail moves from a sender's mailbox, through local post offices, onto delivery trucks, across the country, and into a recipient's mailbox — no single postal worker understands the entire journey, yet the letter arrives. The Internet is both systems at once: the "pipes" are electrons and photons; the "postal service" is packets, routers, and addresses. Every chapter of this site is really just a more precise version of one of those two stories.

Did you know?

When you video-call someone on another continent, your data almost certainly does not go through a satellite. According to TeleGeography, satellites account for only about 0.37% of U.S. international Internet capacity — the overwhelming majority of the world's data crosses oceans through fiber-optic cables lying on the sea floor. We'll see exactly how those cables work in Chapter 12.

How each chapter is built

Every chapter on this site follows the same three-pass structure, so you can choose how deep you want to go:

  1. The concept. A plain-language explanation and an analogy, aimed at anyone — no jargon assumed.
  2. The mechanism. What's actually happening at the packet, protocol, or kernel level — the part that satisfies an engineer's "okay but how, exactly" instinct.
  3. The lab. A hands-on exercise using real tools — Wireshark, tcpdump, curl, scapy, raw sockets, iptables, and friends — so you can watch the concept happen on your own machine, not just read about it.

Diagrams, comparison tables, and "did-you-know" callouts like the one above appear throughout. Every non-obvious technical claim is grounded in a cited, authoritative source — RFCs, vendor documentation, or established references — so you can verify anything that surprises you. And to be direct about the one boundary this site holds firmly: everything here is public, legal, defensive knowledge. Nothing on this site is a guide to attacking a network you don't own or have permission to test.

The road ahead

The chapters build on each other roughly bottom-up — from physics, to wires, to packets, to protocols, to planet-scale infrastructure — but each one is written to stand alone if you already know the basics and just want the deep dive on one layer.
