Chapter 5 · Making Delivery Reliable (Or Not)

Transport Layer

IP promises nothing except a best effort. Packets can arrive out of order, duplicated, or not at all. The transport layer is where the Internet decides whether that's actually a problem — and builds the machinery to fix it when it is.

⏱ 20–25 min read 📋 Prerequisites: Chapter 4 📍 Chapter 5 of 12

What IP doesn't promise, and one thing it doesn't even have

Chapter 4 got a packet from any device to any other, one router hop at a time. But IP's contract is deliberately thin: it's best-effort delivery. Nothing in the IP layer guarantees packets arrive in the order they were sent, guarantees they arrive at all, or even guarantees they don't arrive twice. Routers drop packets constantly under congestion, and IP shrugs and moves on. Something else has to decide whether that's acceptable.

There's also a gap IP never tries to fill: an IP address identifies a machine, not a specific conversation happening on it. A single server can run a web server, a mail server, and an SSH daemon simultaneously, all reachable at the same IP address. The transport layer adds port numbers — a 16-bit number tacked onto every connection — so an incoming packet can be handed to the right application. An IP address plus a port number together is called a socket, and it's the actual endpoint every network conversation happens between.

UDP: send it and hope

UDP (User Datagram Protocol) is the transport layer at its most honest: it adds a source port, a destination port, and a checksum to catch corruption — and nothing else. No handshake, no ordering, no retransmission. If a UDP packet is lost, UDP will never notice, and neither will the network. Whoever's using it has to decide for themselves whether that matters.

That sounds like a downside until you look at what actually chooses UDP: DNS lookups (Chapter 6) are small enough to fit in one packet, so there's nothing to reassemble and a lost query is trivially cheap to just ask again. Live video and voice calls choose UDP too, for the opposite reason most people expect — a dropped video frame that arrives late is useless, so retransmitting it would only add delay to a stream that's supposed to be live. Better to drop it and move on to the next frame than hold up everything waiting for a repeat that's already obsolete by the time it arrives.

📮 Analogy: UDP is a postcard

Drop a postcard in a mailbox and it either arrives or it doesn't — nobody signs for it, nobody tells you if it got lost, and you get no confirmation either way. That's UDP: fast, low-overhead, and entirely indifferent to whether any individual message actually makes it.

TCP: reliable delivery over an unreliable network

TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) takes the opposite approach: it turns IP's best-effort delivery into what looks, from the application's point of view, like a reliable, ordered stream of bytes — entirely through software bookkeeping layered on top of the same unreliable packets UDP uses. Every TCP connection starts with a three-way handshake:

  • SYNclient: "I'd like to start a connection, here's my starting sequence number"1
  • SYN-ACKserver: "acknowledged, here's mine"2
  • ACKclient: "acknowledged — connection established"3

Those sequence numbers aren't cosmetic — every byte sent over the connection is numbered, so the receiver can detect gaps, put out-of-order packets back in order, and send back acknowledgments (ACKs) confirming what's arrived. If the sender doesn't see an ACK for data within a timeout, it assumes the packet was lost and retransmits it. The entire illusion of a reliable connection is built from nothing but numbered packets and a willingness to resend.

Two more mechanisms round out TCP: flow control, where the receiver advertises how much buffer space it has left so a fast sender doesn't overwhelm a slow receiver, and congestion control, where the sender deliberately starts slow and ramps up its sending rate gradually — backing off sharply the moment it detects packet loss, on the assumption that loss means the network itself is overloaded, not just the receiver.

⚡ Did you know?

TCP didn't always back off under congestion. In October 1986, parts of the early Internet suffered what's now called a "congestion collapse" — throughput on one link dropped by a factor of roughly 1,000, as retransmissions of already-lost packets piled on top of the congestion that caused the loss in the first place, making everything worse. Engineer Van Jacobson's fix, published in 1988, introduced the slow-start and congestion-avoidance algorithms that make TCP back off aggressively the instant it detects loss. In some modernized form, that 1988 fix is still running inside nearly every TCP connection on Earth today.

🧪 Try it yourself: watch a handshake happen

Start a Wireshark capture, then in a terminal:

curl -v https://example.com

Filter the capture for tcp.port == 443 and you'll see the exact three packets above — SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK — before a single byte of the actual HTTPS request goes out. Everything covered in this chapter is sitting right there in three small packets.

🧪 Try it yourself: feel the difference between TCP and UDP

Open two terminals. In the first, start a UDP listener:

nc -u -l 9999

In the second, send it something:

echo "hello" | nc -u <host> 9999

Notice there's no connection setup at all — the message just goes. Now try the same thing without -u (plain TCP) and watch Wireshark: you'll see the three-way handshake happen before your "hello" is allowed to send, and a graceful close (FIN packets) after.

What's next

We now have two kinds of pipe: a reliable ordered stream (TCP) and a fast, disposable datagram (UDP). Neither one, on its own, means anything to a human — nobody browses the web by sending raw TCP bytes by hand. Chapter 6 covers the application-layer protocols built on top of these transports: HTTP and HTTPS, DNS, SSH, WebSockets, and email — the layer where the Internet finally starts looking like something you actually use.
