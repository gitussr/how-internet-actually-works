Chapter 9 · When the Network Gets Tiny

IoT & Radio Devices

Not every radio device is trying to join the Internet. Some just need to blink a light, ring a doorbell, or bounce a signal off an aircraft. This chapter covers the small stuff, and the radio use that isn't really networking at all.

15–20 min read Prerequisites: Chapters 2 & 8 Chapter 9 of 12

A different constraint: not speed, but survival

Every wireless technology so far in this site has chased more bandwidth: Wi-Fi, then cellular, then 5G, each one squeezing more data through the air per second. IoT devices mostly don't care about any of that. A soil moisture sensor in a field, a smart light bulb, a fitness tracker's heart-rate monitor — these need to run for months or years on a battery smaller than a coin, or cost pennies to manufacture. Given that constraint, burning power to join a Wi-Fi network and hold a full TCP/IP stack open is often not an option at all. A whole family of protocols exists specifically to solve for battery life and cost instead of speed.

Short-range, low-power IoT protocols

Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) is a stripped-down variant of Bluetooth built to sleep almost all the time and wake only for short, infrequent bursts — a fitness tracker syncing your heart rate doesn't need a persistent connection, just a periodic check-in. Zigbee and 6LoWPAN take a different approach for home automation: devices form a mesh, relaying each other's messages so a signal can hop from a sensor in the garage to a hub in the living room without either one needing enough power to reach that far directly. LoRaWAN goes the opposite direction entirely — very low data rate, but kilometers of range on minimal power, suited to things like agricultural sensors spread across a farm.

Analogy: IoT traffic is raindrops, not a river

A video call needs a steady, continuous flow of data — a river. An IoT sensor sends a tiny reading every few minutes and then goes back to sleep — a raindrop, individually tiny, occasionally missed without consequence, and never expected to form a continuous stream. Nearly every design choice in this chapter's protocols follows from optimizing for raindrops instead of rivers.

Try it yourself: see the raindrops around you

Most laptops and phones can scan for nearby Bluetooth Low Energy advertisements — the periodic "here I am" broadcasts BLE devices send out even when not connected to anything: 

# Linux, using BlueZ:
sudo bluetoothctl
[bluetooth]# scan on

Leave it running for a minute in almost any populated space and you'll likely see several devices — headphones, fitness trackers, smart home sensors — quietly broadcasting their presence to no one in particular, exactly like the raindrops in the analogy above.

Remote controls: the "radio" device that isn't

A television remote doesn't use radio at all — it uses infrared light, just below the frequencies your eyes can see. Press a button and it blinks a specific pattern of pulses at an IR LED; the TV's sensor reads that pattern and matches it to a command. There's no addressing, no acknowledgment, no network of any kind — just a one-way blast of light that only works if the remote is pointed roughly at the receiver. It's about as close to "no protocol at all" as a wireless device gets. Garage door openers and car key fobs, by contrast, use actual radio (not light), which is why they work without a clear line of sight the way an IR remote doesn't.

Walkie-talkies: radio with no network underneath it

A walkie-talkie strips away everything this site has built up chapter by chapter — no IP address, no MAC address, no handshake, not even a tower. Two units simply transmit and receive on the same shared radio frequency, and whoever is listening on that frequency hears whoever is transmitting. It's the most direct form of long-distance communication possible: a wave, sent into the air, with nothing standing between the sender and anyone tuned to listen. Every layer this site has covered since Chapter 3 exists to add exactly what a walkie-talkie doesn't have: addressing, routing, and privacy.

Radar: not communication at all

Everything else in this chapter sends information to someone. Radar — Radio Detection and Ranging — does something different entirely: it transmits a pulse and listens for its own echo bouncing off a distant object, using the round-trip delay to calculate distance. Nobody is receiving a message; the "message" is being used purely as a measuring stick.

Did you know?

The math behind radar is the exact same physics behind Chapter 2's fiber-optic latency floor, just measured through open air instead of glass: distance equals the speed of light multiplied by the round-trip time, divided by two. Air traffic control, weather radar, and a car's collision-avoidance system are all, underneath, running the same simple equation Chapter 2 used to calculate the minimum possible latency between New York and London.
