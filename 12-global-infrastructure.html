Chapter 12 · The View From Orbit

Global Infrastructure

Every layer of this site has led here: thousands of independently owned networks, meeting at neutral exchange points, connected by strands of glass lying on the ocean floor — the actual physical shape of "the Internet."

15–20 min read Prerequisites: Chapters 2 & 4 Chapter 12 of 12

Tiers: not every network is equal

Chapter 4 introduced Autonomous Systems and BGP — the mechanism networks use to tell each other what they can reach. Zoom out, and those tens of thousands of ASes settle into a rough hierarchy. Tier-1 networks sit at the top: by the common industry definition, a Tier-1 network can reach every other network on the Internet without ever paying anyone for transit — it's earned that reach entirely through peering. Names like AT&T, NTT, and Lumen show up on most lists of Tier-1 operators. Tier-2 networks peer with some networks but still pay for transit to reach the rest; Tier-3 networks — often the regional ISP actually wiring up your house — mostly just buy transit from someone bigger and don't peer directly with much of anyone.

Peering vs. transit: free vs. paid

Two networks that exchange roughly similar amounts of traffic with each other can agree to just connect directly and carry each other's traffic for free — peering — because it saves both sides money and both benefit equally. When traffic is one-sided, or a smaller network simply can't reach most of the Internet through peering alone, it pays a larger network for transit — essentially renting the bigger network's reach.

CostUsually free (settlement-free)Paid

IXPs: where networks actually meet

Peering with a hundred different networks one-by-one would mean running a hundred separate physical connections. An Internet Exchange Point (IXP) solves that: it's a physical facility with a shared switching fabric where many networks connect once, and can then peer with any other network present — some of the largest IXPs, like DE-CIX in Frankfurt or AMS-IX in Amsterdam, host well over a thousand connected networks trading traffic directly, entirely bypassing the wider transit Internet for anything local to that exchange.

Analogy: an IXP is a roundabout, not a thousand private driveways

If every pair of nearby towns built its own dedicated private road to every other town, the result would be an unmanageable tangle. Build one roundabout where all the roads meet instead, and any town can reach any other with one well-defined connection. An IXP is that roundabout for networks — meet once, reach everyone else who also showed up.

Submarine cables: the actual physical Internet

This site opened with a number worth returning to now that every layer beneath it has been explained: satellites carry only a sliver of international Internet traffic — TeleGeography puts satellite's share of U.S. international capacity at roughly 0.37%. Essentially all the rest travels through submarine fiber-optic cables, of which more than 1.5 million kilometers are currently active worldwide, laid across ocean floors by purpose-built cable ships and stitched into the same fiber physics covered all the way back in Chapter 2.

That's not a coincidental callback — it's the same equation. Chapter 2 calculated a physical latency floor of about 28 milliseconds one-way between New York and London, set purely by the speed of light in glass. Every transatlantic cable route on Earth is engineered as close to a straight line between landing stations as the ocean floor allows, for exactly that reason: there's a hard physical minimum, and the entire point of the engineering is to get as close to it as possible.

Did you know?

The biggest threat to submarine cables isn't sabotage or sharks — it's fishing trawlers and ship anchors. Industry fault data has consistently shown that the large majority of submarine cable faults come from ordinary maritime activity accidentally snagging or dragging across a cable on the seabed. It's a genuinely mundane risk to infrastructure that carries the overwhelming majority of the planet's international data — which is exactly why cable routes and landing sites are planned around shipping lanes and fishing zones as a routine part of the engineering.

Try it yourself: feel the physical distance
traceroute example-server-in-another-continent.com
ping -c 20 example-server-in-another-continent.com

Compare the round-trip time you measure against Chapter 2's math: double the one-way speed-of-light-in-fiber estimate for the approximate distance involved, and you have the physical floor — the absolute best any connection could ever do along that route. A well-routed real connection usually lands within a factor of two or three of that floor; a much larger gap suggests the traffic is taking a far less direct path than the cable map would suggest.

Data centers and "regions": where all of this actually leads

Chapter 7 covered data centers and VPCs from the inside. From the outside, a cloud provider's "region" is simply a cluster of data centers connected by the provider's own private fiber, itself connected to the wider Internet at IXPs and through peering and transit relationships exactly like the ones described in this chapter. Every layer in this site — electron, cable, frame, packet, segment, request, server — ultimately terminates somewhere in a building like the ones Chapter 7 described, reached by infrastructure exactly like the ones this chapter described.

None of these twelve layers know the others exist. A transistor has no concept of a web page; a submarine cable has no concept of a password. And yet, every time you load anything at all, all twelve cooperate perfectly, in milliseconds, without ever being introduced. That was the entire trick this site set out to make visible back in the introduction — and now you've seen every piece of how it's done.
